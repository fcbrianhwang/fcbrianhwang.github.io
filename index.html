Derivatives are where crypto is going. Most compliance programs aren't built for it.

The spot trading model is maturing. Spreads have compressed, margins are thin and the retail-driven volume that fuelled the 2020-2021 cycle hasn't come back the same way. The exchanges with serious long-term ambitions have already started building the next thing: futures, options and perpetual contracts that make simple spot trading look like a starting point, not the destination.

Coinbase's acquisition of FairX in 2022 and the launch of Coinbase Financial Markets was a clear signal. CFTC-regulated futures, options on Bitcoin and Ethereum, institutional-grade infrastructure. The offshore perpetuals market, dominated by Binance, Bybit and OKX, has been running this playbook for years. The question now is whether regulated venues can build it compliantly faster than offshore venues can legitimize themselves.

The compliance problems aren't hypothetical. The AML layer still applies, but a market integrity layer becomes equally critical and most crypto compliance programs aren't built for it. Spoofing, placing large orders with no intention to fill them to move price, is a CFTC enforcement priority that barely exists in traditional transaction monitoring logic. Funding rate manipulation in perpetual contracts, where large leveraged positions push rates in a profitable direction, is a typology most TM systems can't detect. Wash trading looks different in derivatives than it does in spot, harder to surface and more consequential when missed.

What regulated exchanges building derivatives products genuinely need is a compliance architecture that spans financial crime and market integrity simultaneously. Most crypto compliance programs are built for the first layer only. Traditional finance compliance teams understand the second but lack crypto-native context. The institutions that figure out how to combine both will be the ones regulators actually trust to run a derivatives product at scale.

That infrastructure is still being built. The compliance professionals who understand what it needs to look like are in short supply.

Prediction markets went mainstream. The compliance framework didn't follow.

Polymarket crossed $1B in trading volume during the 2024 US election cycle. Kalshi won its court battle with the CFTC over political event contracts. What looked like a fringe product two years ago is now a legitimate asset class and one that existing compliance frameworks were never designed to handle.

Prediction markets are, at their core, binary outcome contracts on real-world events. Will this candidate win the election? Will this company get acquired? Will this regulatory decision go a certain way? The product is elegant. The compliance problem it creates is not.

The most underappreciated risk isn't AML, it's information asymmetry exploitation, the prediction market equivalent of insider trading. Someone with material non-public information placing large positions on a political outcome, a corporate event or a regulatory decision before it's announced. Traditional financial markets have decades of case law and surveillance infrastructure around this. Prediction markets have almost none.

The AML risk is real but takes a different shape. Large binary positions structured across multiple accounts can effectively guarantee a payout on one side while obscuring the source of funds on the other. It's a layering mechanism that sits outside the transaction patterns most monitoring systems are calibrated to find.

Then there's the oracle problem unique to decentralised prediction markets. The resolution mechanism, the data source that determines who won, can itself be manipulated. That's not just a technical risk, it's a compliance and market integrity risk with no clean parallel in traditional finance.

Regulatory classification remains genuinely unsettled. Are these securities? Commodity contracts? Something else entirely? Kalshi's CFTC win resolved one question and opened several others. For compliance professionals, the absence of a clear framework isn't an excuse to wait, it's an argument for building programs flexible enough to absorb whatever classification eventually lands.

Prediction markets are going to be part of the regulated crypto landscape. The compliance infrastructure needs to start taking them seriously now.

On pig butchering and why TM controls keep missing it

The fraud doesn't start with crypto. It starts with a text message.

By the time a victim deposits funds into an exchange, they've already been in a "relationship" with their scammer for weeks, sometimes months. They believe they're investing alongside a trusted contact. They're not panicked, they're excited. And that's exactly what makes pig butchering so difficult to catch with traditional transaction monitoring controls.

Most TM systems are built around anomaly detection, a customer suddenly moving large sums, hitting velocity limits, transacting at unusual hours. But pig-butchering victims don't look anomalous. They look engaged. They deposit regularly, respond to KYC requests promptly, sometimes even contact support proactively. The fraud lives in the destination, not the behaviour.

What's actually working is behavioural layering, looking at the combination of onboarding recency, referral chain patterns, withdrawal destination age and cross-platform intelligence. The most effective signals aren't individual alerts. They're clusters: a new account, a referral chain tied to previously flagged addresses, a withdrawal to a wallet that received funds from three other exchange accounts in the past 30 days.

No TM system catches pig butchering in real time. The goal is earlier detection, better intelligence-sharing between platforms and faster victim notification. The exchanges winning on this aren't just running better rules, they're treating it as an intelligence problem, not a monitoring problem.

VASP registration is good policy. Compliance maturity takes longer.

Canada was late to regulating virtual asset service providers. That's not a controversial statement, it's something any crypto compliance professional working in this market before 2024 felt every time they filed an STR on a transaction that a registered VASP elsewhere would have already flagged.

The VASP registration requirements changed that. For the first time, Canadian crypto platforms have a defined regulatory baseline, AML/ATF programs, reporting obligations, record-keeping requirements. As someone who spent years executing those obligations before they were formally codified, I think FINTRAC got the framework mostly right.

What concerns me is the implementation gap.

Registration isn't the same as maturity. A platform can register with FINTRAC and still run a transaction monitoring system that fires on volume thresholds but misses typology-based patterns entirely. They can file LVCTRs on time and still have no meaningful EDD program for high-risk clients. The framework sets a floor. It doesn't guarantee the ceiling is anywhere near adequate.

The platforms genuinely ahead aren't just meeting FINTRAC's minimum requirements, they're treating regulatory obligations as a baseline, not a finish line. That means real blockchain analytics capabilities, proper escalation pipelines for complex cases and investigators who can actually work production orders alongside law enforcement.

Canada's crypto compliance ecosystem is maturing. But there's a meaningful gap between the platforms that registered to operate and the platforms actually built to catch financial crime. That gap is where most of the risk lives right now.

What actually changes when you use AI in a live AML workflow

Everyone in compliance is talking about AI. Most of the conversation is either hype or fear. Here's something more useful: what it actually looks like to integrate generative AI into a live AML investigation workflow, with real human oversight.

I started using AI tools in my investigations at Newton. Not to replace judgment, to accelerate the parts of investigation work that are time-intensive but not judgment-intensive. Drafting STR narratives from structured case notes. Summarising long transaction histories into a concise chronology. Cross-referencing regulatory guidance against filing thresholds. Tasks that used to take 20-30 minutes per case now take five.

What it cannot do is what matters most. It can't evaluate whether a behaviour pattern constitutes reasonable grounds to suspect money laundering. It can't weigh the credibility of a customer explanation against the red flags in their transaction history. It can't decide whether to escalate to law enforcement or close with enhanced monitoring. Those calls require a trained investigator who understands the regulatory obligations, the evidentiary standards and the institutional context.

The right mental model isn't "AI as an analyst." It's "AI as a very fast research assistant who never gets tired." The oversight stays human. The judgment stays human. The regulatory responsibility definitely stays human.

What AI has changed for me is capacity. When it handles the structured, repeatable parts of investigation workflow, investigators can go deeper on the cases that actually need depth. That's a real improvement in compliance quality, not just efficiency.
