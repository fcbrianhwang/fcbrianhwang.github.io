North Korea just robbed Binance of $6.3 billion. I'm not sure the industry is ready for what that means.

Look, I've been in crypto compliance long enough to remember when a $100M hack felt catastrophic. Last month, $6.36 billion left Binance in roughly twenty minutes. It's the biggest crypto theft in history and I'm still processing what that actually means for everyone working in this space.

The FBI confirmed North Korea's TraderTraitor group was responsible, the same crew behind the Bybit hack eight months earlier. Only this time, they took four times as much. If that number doesn't stop you in your tracks, I don't know what will.

Here's what gets me: Binance caught 57 suspected North Korean intrusion attempts in 2026 alone. Fifty-seven. And the fifty-eighth one got through anyway. That's not carelessness, that's the reality of defending against a state-funded operation with basically unlimited time and patience.

The coordinated freeze response was genuinely impressive. Within hours, stablecoin issuers froze roughly $3 billion of the stolen funds. That's real progress from how these situations went even two years ago. But $3 billion frozen also means $3.3 billion that wasn't. Gone. Across ten blockchains. In twenty minutes.

The honest truth is that most compliance programs aren't built for nation-state threats. The frameworks we use, transaction monitoring, filing suspicious activity reports, verifying customer identities, were designed to catch criminals. Not governments. Not adversaries who study your defences for months before striking once, perfectly. Two attacks of this scale in one year should change how the industry thinks about what a compliance program is actually supposed to do.

Crypto exchanges aren't just exchanges anymore. The compliance problem that comes with that.

For a while, crypto was relatively simple from a product standpoint. You could buy Bitcoin. You could sell it. That was pretty much the whole menu.

That world is over. The major platforms are now offering futures, options and perpetual contracts, the same complex instruments that have existed in traditional financial markets for decades. Coinbase's acquisition of FairX in 2022 was an early signal of where things were heading. Their derivatives exchange is now regulated by the CFTC, which is a different regulator from the ones most crypto compliance teams are used to dealing with.

Quick explainer: a futures contract lets you agree today to buy or sell something at a set price on a future date. Options give you the right (but not the obligation) to do the same. Perpetual contracts are unique to crypto, they work like futures but never expire and there's a daily fee that keeps the price anchored to the underlying asset. These products let traders take bigger positions with less money upfront, which makes them popular and also makes them riskier.

The compliance problem is that derivatives open up manipulation tactics that don't exist in simple buy-and-sell trading. Spoofing, placing large fake orders to push the price, then cancelling before they fill, is a CFTC enforcement priority. Funding rate manipulation in perpetuals is another one. Most crypto compliance programs weren't built to detect either of these.

What the industry needs is a compliance setup that covers both financial crime (the AML side) and market integrity (the manipulation side) at the same time. Right now, most teams are strong on one or the other, not both. The platforms that figure out how to combine them will be better positioned when regulators inevitably start paying closer attention to the derivatives side of the business.

Prediction markets are bigger than you think. The rules haven't caught up.

If you bet on who'd win the 2024 US election on Polymarket, you were part of something bigger than most people realized at the time. That platform alone crossed $1 billion in trading volume during election week. Kalshi, a competing platform, just won a court battle allowing it to offer contracts on political events in the US. What felt fringe a couple years ago is now a real financial market.

Quick explainer if you're new to this: a prediction market lets you bet on real-world outcomes. Will this team win? Will this bill pass? Will this company get acquired? You buy a contract that pays out if you're right. The price reflects what everyone collectively thinks the odds are. Simple enough.

The compliance gaps, though, are not simple. The most obvious one: what happens when someone bets on an event they know the outcome of before anyone else does? In stock markets, that's insider trading and it's illegal. In prediction markets, there's no equivalent rule yet. Someone with advance knowledge of a political decision or regulatory announcement could place a large bet and clean up, legally, for now.

There's also a money laundering angle that gets less attention. You can structure bets across multiple accounts to guarantee a payout on one side while hiding where the money originally came from. It's a layering technique that existing monitoring systems aren't looking for because nobody designed those systems with prediction markets in mind.

Regulatory classification is still unsettled, are these securities? Commodity contracts? Nobody's fully agreed yet. That uncertainty isn't an excuse to wait. If anything, it's an argument for getting ahead of this now, before the enforcement actions start and everyone scrambles to catch up.

Someone stole $1.5 billion from Bybit. What I think about when I think about stories like this.

On February 21, 2025, hackers stole $1.5 billion from crypto exchange Bybit. The FBI confirmed North Korea's TraderTraitor group was responsible within five days of the attack. For context, that's more than many mid-sized banks hold in total deposits. Gone in one day.

The post-hack response was actually impressive. Analytics firms mapped the wallet clusters almost immediately. The FBI published specific Ethereum addresses connected to the theft within days. Exchanges and stablecoin issuers coordinated freezes faster than I'd seen before. The on-chain intelligence was there, nearly in real time.

But here's what that fast response also tells you: the tools to track this kind of theft after the fact are genuinely getting better. What hasn't kept pace is the ability to prevent it in the first place.

Most AML programs are designed to catch financial criminals, people structuring transactions to hide illegal income or scammers laundering money through exchanges. That's hard enough. North Korea's hackers operate completely differently. They're patient. They study their targets for months. They hit once, decisively, then immediately spread funds across dozens of wallets to outrun freeze attempts. Traditional compliance controls don't catch that. They weren't designed to.

The question the industry needs to start taking seriously isn't whether your compliance program meets regulatory requirements. It probably does. The question is whether it's designed for the actual threat environment and that threat environment now includes nation-states with serious technical resources and nothing but time.

The fraud your bank's monitoring system won't catch.

Pig butchering doesn't start with cryptocurrency. It starts with a wrong-number text message.

You get a message from someone you don't know. They're apologetic, friendly, curious about your day. Over the following weeks, they become a regular presence, checking in, sharing things about themselves, eventually introducing you to an investment opportunity that's supposedly been making them serious money. By the time crypto enters the picture, you've known this person for two or three months. You trust them. And that trust is exactly what the scam depends on.

The name sounds jarring and it is. It refers to the practice of fattening up a pig before slaughter, except the "pig" is the victim and the fattening is the relationship-building phase that happens before any money changes hands.

Here's why exchanges struggle to catch it: the people getting scammed don't look like fraud victims. They look like engaged customers. They onboard normally, pass identity checks, respond to messages and deposit money in growing amounts. The fraud is in where the money goes, not in how the customer behaves. Most transaction monitoring systems are designed to flag unusual behaviour. Pig butchering victims don't behave unusually, so the alerts don't fire until it's often too late.

What's starting to work is looking beyond the customer's own behaviour, checking where the money lands, whether that destination address has been linked to other reports, how old the receiving wallet is. It's less about monitoring one customer and more about mapping a wider network. It's as much an intelligence problem as a compliance problem. And the industry is still catching up.

Canada made crypto exchanges register with regulators. That's actually a big deal.

For a long time, if you wanted to run a crypto exchange serving Canadians, there wasn't much of a formal process. You could operate, offer trading, hold customer funds, without the kind of regulatory oversight that a bank or investment dealer would face.

That changed. As of 2024, any business exchanging or transferring crypto for Canadians has to register with FINTRAC (Canada's financial intelligence unit) as a money services business. That means having an anti-money laundering program, reporting suspicious transactions, keeping proper records, the same framework that applies to currency exchanges and money transfer businesses. It's called the PCMLTFA, if you want the full name.

As someone who's been executing those obligations at Canadian crypto platforms for years, often before they were formally required, the registration rules feel overdue. And mostly right.

What I mean by "mostly": registration sets a floor. It tells platforms, here's the minimum. Have a compliance program. Report suspicious transactions. Keep records. That's genuinely important. What it doesn't guarantee is that any of it is actually good. A platform can register, check all the boxes and still have a transaction monitoring system that fires on volume but misses patterns that actually matter.

Registration is a start. The platforms I'd actually trust to catch financial crime aren't the ones just meeting the requirements. They're the ones treating the requirements as the beginning of the conversation, not the end.

FTX collapsed. It wasn't really a crypto story.

In November 2022, FTX, one of the largest crypto exchanges in the world, imploded. Around $8 billion in customer funds vanished. Sam Bankman-Fried, the founder, was eventually convicted of fraud and sentenced to 25 years in prison.

You've probably heard a version of this story framed as evidence that crypto is too risky, too unregulated, too wild to be taken seriously. I think that framing misses what actually happened.

FTX didn't fail because it was a crypto company. It failed because nobody was watching the money.

The basic mechanics of the collapse were embarrassingly old-fashioned. Customer funds, money belonging to people trading on the platform, were being quietly moved into Alameda Research, an affiliated trading firm that Bankman-Fried also controlled. Those funds were then used to cover losses, make investments and fund other spending. This isn't a crypto problem. This is what happens when you don't keep client money separate from company money. When there's no independent audit. When the person running compliance reports to the same person doing the trades. These are basic governance failures that have existed in financial fraud long before blockchain existed.

The lesson isn't "crypto is dangerous." The lesson is that the fundamentals matter, no matter what you're trading.

Binance paid $4.3 billion in fines. What I actually took from that.

On November 21, 2023, Binance pleaded guilty to violating US anti-money laundering laws and agreed to pay over $4.3 billion, the largest settlement in the history of crypto. Changpeng Zhao, the founder and CEO (a Canadian citizen), stepped down and pleaded guilty personally. $3.4 billion went to FinCEN (the US financial crimes agency) and $968 million went to OFAC (the sanctions enforcement arm of the Treasury). The settlement also came with a five-year independent compliance monitor, essentially an outside overseer watching everything Binance does for the next five years.

The violations weren't exotic. According to the DOJ and Treasury, Binance allowed transactions connected to terrorist groups including Hamas and Al Qaeda, ransomware attackers and users in sanctioned countries like Iran and North Korea to pass through without being flagged or reported. Not because their compliance team missed it. Because deliberate decisions were made to avoid building the systems that would have caught it.

That's the part I keep coming back to. This wasn't a capability failure. Binance had the resources and the people. They made a business decision that compliance infrastructure would slow growth and they chose growth.

For anyone working in this space, the settlement documents are worth reading. The DOJ and Treasury laid out exactly what they expected Binance to have in place and didn't. It's as close to a regulatory instruction manual as the industry has ever been given publicly. The question is whether anyone uses it.

Forty billion dollars disappeared in a week. Here's what I think actually went wrong.

In May 2022, two cryptocurrencies called TerraUSD and LUNA essentially ceased to exist within about seven days. At their peak, they were worth something in the range of $40 billion combined. By the end of that week, nearly all of it was gone.

Short explanation of how: TerraUSD was a "stablecoin", a token designed to always be worth exactly $1. But unlike most stablecoins, it wasn't backed by actual dollars in a bank. It was backed by an algorithm and by another token (LUNA) that was supposed to absorb volatility. When people started selling TerraUSD in large volumes, the algorithm tried to defend the peg by creating more LUNA. That flooded the market, crashed LUNA's price and made the whole system collapse faster. It's a feedback loop that's obvious in hindsight. Do Kwon, the founder, was later charged with fraud and sentenced in December 2025.

What I keep thinking about is the 20% annual return that the Terra ecosystem's savings product, Anchor Protocol, was offering. Twenty percent. Guaranteed. In a world where most savings accounts pay under 1%.

That should've been a red flag visible from space. Returns that high, sustained at scale, don't come from genuine economic activity. They come from somewhere else, usually from new investor money subsidising payments to existing investors. It's the same basic structure as every Ponzi scheme that's ever existed, just wrapped in blockchain terminology.

The investors who lost money weren't all naive. Many were sophisticated. But they were operating in a space where the normal guardrails, the things that make a bank or investment firm explain risks clearly and check whether you can afford to lose, simply didn't exist. That's the real gap. Not the technology. The absence of basic consumer protection.

I started using AI in my investigations. Here's what actually changed.

There's a lot of noise right now about AI replacing analysts. I've been using generative AI tools in my AML work for a while now and that's not what's happening, at least not in my experience.

What it's actually changed is the parts of investigation work that are time-consuming but don't really require deep judgment. Writing up case notes into a formal STR narrative (that's a Suspicious Transaction Report, a document you file with the financial regulator when you think a transaction might be linked to money laundering). Turning a messy transaction history into a clear summary. Cross-checking regulatory guidance to confirm the right filing threshold. Those tasks used to eat up 20-30 minutes per case. Now they take five.

What it doesn't replace: deciding whether something is actually suspicious. Reading a customer's explanation against the red flags in their account history. Knowing when to escalate to law enforcement versus when to close a case with a note in the file. That judgment, the actual compliance decision, is entirely human. And it needs to stay that way.

The right way to think about it isn't "AI analyst." It's more like having a very organized assistant who handles the paperwork while you do the thinking. The writing gets done faster. The summaries are cleaner. And because the administrative stuff takes less time, I can go deeper on the cases that actually need depth.

That's a real improvement. Not a replacement. The judgment at the centre of this work stays with people and I think that's exactly how it should be.
